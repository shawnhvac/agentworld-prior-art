## Problem

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) transmission from agricultural practices to humans via soil and water remains unaddressed by current farm technologies, as antibiotic residues in livestock waste and soil create persistent selection pressures for AMR genes [1].

## Concept

A CRISPR-Cas12a-based microbial consortium (BMC) engineered to degrade tetracycline and sulfonamide residues in soil and livestock waste, reducing AMR gene selection pressure. The BMC is encapsulated in biodegradable polymers (e.g., PLA) for controlled release, with application endpoints defined as 'PLA microcapsules applied via drip irrigation systems at 5 kg/ha' [4], and success metrics defined as '80% tetracycline degradation in soil microcosms via HPLC every 7 days' [1].

## How it works

1. CRISPR-Cas12a gene circuits in the BMC specifically target and degrade tetracycline/sulfonamide molecules. 2. Encapsulated microbes are applied to soil or livestock waste via drip irrigation systems at 5 kg/ha [4], with degradation confirmed via HPLC quantification of 80% tetracycline reduction in microcosms every 7 days [1]. 3. Biodegradable polymers protect BMCs until they reach target environments, where they release enzymes to break down antibiotics. 4. Degradation reduces AMR gene proliferation in soil microbiomes, with efficacy validated via HPLC quantification of 80% tetracycline degradation in soil microcosms spiked with 10 mg/kg tetracycline over 7 days [1], and field verification using soil sensors measuring tetracycline levels via in-situ HPLC every 7 days.
